## Introduction

The project aims to provide a tool for observing the trajectories of charged particles, treated as point particles, moving through electromagnetic fields. The simulator is intended for non-relativistic motion and employs the Explicit Euler method for numerical integration.

---

## Coordinate System

For the simulation, a three-dimensional Cartesian coordinate system is used, with the Z-axis aligned with the beam propagation direction.

```text
X → transverse axis

Y → transverse axis

Z → primary beam direction
```
---

### Electromagnetic Fields


For simplicity and user-friendliness, the option to describe electromagnetic fields in 6 components, representing the electric and magnetic field components separately, has been chosen without implementing automatic treatment of time-varying fields.

```text
Ex = f(x,y,z,t)          Bx = f(x,y,z,t)

Ey = f(x,y,z,t)          By = f(x,y,z,t)

Ez = f(x,y,z,t)          Bz = f(x,y,z,t)
```

##  Euler Method 

The Euler method is a first-order numerical method for solving ordinary differential equations (ODEs). It is based on the idea of approximating the solution of the ODE at a given timestep by using the solution at the previous timestep. The method is simple and easy to implement, but it has a limited accuracy and can be unstable for certain types of equations.

The Euler method is described by the following formula:

$$ \vec{x}(t+\Delta t)=\vec{x}(t)+\vec{v}(t)\,\Delta t $$

Where 'x' is the position of the particle, 'v' is the velocity, 't' is the time, and 'At' is the timestep.

---

## Acceleration Calculation and Application

The acceleration of each particle is computed from the Lorentz force equation.

The Lorentz force acting on a charged particle is given by
$$ \vec{F} = q \left( \vec{E} + \vec{v} \times \vec{B} \right) $$
where:
$$ \vec{E}= \begin{bmatrix} E_x\\ E_y\\ E_z \end{bmatrix} \qquad \vec{B}= \begin{bmatrix} B_x\\ B_y\\ B_z \end{bmatrix} \qquad \vec{v}= \begin{bmatrix} v_x\\ v_y\\ v_z \end{bmatrix} $$
since the simulator uses directly: $$ \frac{q}{m}$$
and applying the Lorentz force equation:$$ \vec{a} = \frac{q}{m} \left( \vec{E} + \vec{v} \times \vec{B} \right) $$
this gives us the following formula:$$ \vec{a} = \frac{q}{m} \begin{bmatrix} E_x + v_y B_z - v_z B_y\\ E_y + v_z B_x - v_x B_z\\ E_z + v_x B_y - v_y B_x \end{bmatrix} $$

Once the acceleration has been computed from the Lorentz force, the particle velocity is updated using the Explicit Euler integration method. The velocity at the next timestep is obtained by adding the variation generated by the current acceleration over the interval Δt.
$$\vec{v}(t+\Delta t) = \vec{v}(t) + \vec{a}(t)\,\Delta t$$

## Limitations

Due to the numerical integration method adopted by the simulator, several limitations must be considered.

The Explicit Euler method provides a simple and computationally efficient approximation of particle trajectories, but its accuracy depends strongly on the selected timestep.

As a consequence:

- Very intense interactions occurring over extremely short time intervals may not be accurately resolved and can be partially missed by the integration process.
- The kinetic energy of the particles is numerically approximated rather than computed through an energy-conserving scheme.
- The simulator does not guarantee exact energy conservation, and small numerical errors may accumulate during long simulations.

Reducing the timestep generally improves accuracy at the expense of computational cost.
